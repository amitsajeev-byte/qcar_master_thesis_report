\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter consolidates the quantitative results produced across
Chapters~\ref{ch:sim-stack}--\ref{ch:nav-integration} and evaluates
them directly against the target state defined in
Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}: a Nav2 navigation stack that
reaches goal poses reliably on the physical QCar, with the
sim-to-real gap identified and, where practical, corrected. Results
are presented here without re-opening their diagnosis; interpretation
and generalisation are deferred to Chapter~\ref{ch:summary}.

Table~\ref{tab:results-at-a-glance} gives the headline figure for
each result before the corresponding section restates it in full
detail. Every row here is a forward reference, not a new claim.

\section{Sim-versus-Real Comparison}
\label{sec:results-sim-vs-real}

The same three goals reported above for QCar, a straight
$\approx$\SI{2}{\meter} approach (Goal 1), a curved
$\approx$\ang{90} turn (Goal 2) and a near-\ang{180} reversal (Goal 3),
were also run in simulation. Table~\ref{tab:results-sim-vs-real}
compares the two directly: duration, cusp count, recoveries triggered
and final position/yaw error against the given goal.

Simulation completed every goal faster than hardware (\SI{7.11}{\second}
vs.\ \SI{8.47}{\second}; \SI{11.32}{\second} vs.\ \SI{21.87}{\second};
\SI{16.27}{\second} vs.\ \SI{29.3}{\second}) and reached a smaller
final position error in every case (\SI{0.107}{\meter} vs.\
\SI{0.214}{\meter}; \SI{0.034}{\meter} vs.\ \SI{0.129}{\meter};
\SI{0.10}{\meter} vs.\ \SI{0.239}{\meter}). Yaw error runs the other
way for Goals 2 and 3, where simulation's yaw error (\ang{17.0},
\ang{9.4}) exceeds hardware's (\ang{8.6}, \ang{6.0}). Neither
environment triggered a recovery behaviour for any of the three goals.
Goal 3's executed path also differed in shape: a single cusp in
simulation against four in the hardware run.

a genuine reversal manoeuvre, both succeeded with zero recoveries

and real replanning behaviour, rather than a limitation of the fix
itself.

\section{Navigation Goal-Reaching}
\label{sec:results-goal-reaching}

Closed-loop \texttt{navigate\_to\_pose} goals requiring both straight
driving and real turning were completed successfully on the physical
QCar on multiple occasions across this project. This was confirmed
both by \texttt{bt\_navigator}'s own \texttt{Goal succeeded} status
and, in the final validation of Section~\ref{sec:final-validation}, by
my own direct observation that localisation tracked the
vehicle's real motion closely throughout the goal, rather than
exhibiting the map-shift artefact of Section~\ref{sec:display-lag}. The one
navigation timing measurement available (Table~\ref{tab:display-lag})
was taken under the pre-\texttt{readMode=0} bridge configuration and
should be read as an upper bound on the true post-fix pipeline
latency and stop-coast behaviour during a live goal, not as the final
figure, per the caveat in Section~\ref{sec:display-lag-interpretation}.

The one goal class with a genuine, repeated hardware success count is
the reversal/K-turn manoeuvre of Section~\ref{sec:results-cusp}: 2 of
2 sent goals succeeded with zero recoveries. General straight-line and
gentle-turn goal-reaching, by contrast, is supported only by my own
qualitative assessments above and by individual successful
\texttt{Goal succeeded} events logged during development, not by a
planned, repeated multi-goal trial campaign with a computed success
rate. This is stated plainly here, rather than implied to be
equivalent to the K-turn result and is carried forward as the first
item in Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations}.

Two further general (non-K-turn) goals were run on QCar.
Each was logged in full: given goal, AMCL-estimated
final pose, the planner's path statistics and commanded-vs-actual
velocity over the whole run, not just a qualitative pass/fail
observation.

\subsection{Goal 1, a Straight-Line Approach}
\label{sec:goal1-straight}

The first trial commanded a straight approach: a goal pose of
$(1.978, 0.003, \ang{0})$ in the map frame, \SI{1.871}{\meter} away in
a straight line from the vehicle's pose at goal-send time.
Physically, this was an approximately \SI{2}{\meter} run set up on
the lab floor. \texttt{SmacPlannerHybrid} planned a 29-waypoint, zero-cusp (no
reversal) path of \SI{1.874}{\meter}, essentially the straight line
itself (curvature ratio 1.00$\times$), smoothed by the
cusp-straightener smoother. The goal was accepted at epoch
1788779882.264. \texttt{Goal succeeded} was reported
\SI{8.47}{\second} later with zero recovery behaviours triggered.

\paragraph{Repeated 3$\times$: Real Run-to-Run Variability.}
To check whether Goal~2's single result above was representative, the
identical given goal ($(1.891, -1.775, \ang{-90})$, pulled directly
from that run's own recorded data) was sent three further times on
QCar from the same start position and map, in what turned
out to be this project's final hardware session. All three repeats
also reported \texttt{Goal succeeded}, but with genuine run-to-run
variability. Duration ranged \SIrange{17.8}{60.4}{\second}, some
repeats' planned paths included a reverse-entering cusp that the
original run never needed and position error ranged
\SIrange{0.146}{0.222}{\meter}. No repeat matched the original's
particular combination of fast, clean and accurate. This is a direct,
if small-sample, hardware demonstration of the same real run-to-run
planning and execution variability this project's calibration work
(Chapters~\ref{ch:hardware-bridge}--\ref{ch:calibration}) already
characterises at the actuator level, here visible at the level of
whole-path planning and outcome.

\paragraph{Why Goal~1 and Goal~2 exceed their position tolerance.}
Both Goal~1 and Goal~2 above were reported as \texttt{Goal succeeded}
despite their final AMCL-estimated pose sitting outside the configured
\SI{0.12}{\meter} \texttt{xy\_goal\_tolerance} (\SI{0.214}{\meter} and
\SI{0.129}{\meter} respectively). This is not a new or unexplained
failure mode. It is a hardware confirmation of a tension this
project's own MPPI tuning had already identified between
\texttt{GoalCritic} (pulling the vehicle to converge exactly on the
goal point) and \texttt{PathAlignCritic} (favouring a smooth final
approach along the planned path), weighted in this project's
configuration to favour the latter.
Goal~2's yaw error (\ang{8.6}) remained comfortably inside its own
tolerance, so the discrepancy is specific to final position, not
orientation.

\subsection{Goal 3, a Near-\ang{180} Reversal Goal}
\label{sec:goal3-reversal}

This trial commanded a harder course than Goals~1 and~2:
\SI{3.0}{\meter} straight ahead of the start position, but with the
final orientation reversed \ang{180} from the starting heading.
Because an Ackermann-steered vehicle cannot rotate in place, reaching
a reversed heading genuinely requires a K-turn/loop-out manoeuvre

costmap invalidated it, to a final 22-waypoint, 4-cusp path. The goal
was accepted at epoch 1789046995.425. \texttt{Goal succeeded} was
reported \SI{29.3}{\second} later with zero recovery behaviours
triggered.

\section{Map Quality and Localisation Accuracy}
\label{sec:results-map-localization}

The two general goals above compare a given goal to AMCL's own final
pose estimate. This is a genuine navigation-accuracy result, but not
an independent check of that estimate against physical reality, since
both figures come from the same localisation system. This section
closes that gap directly with a physical ground-truth measurement,
using the same hardware map as the two goals above
(\texttt{maps/qcar\_map\_hardware.yaml}) and, for the localisation
test, the same straight \SI{2}{\meter} goal and start position as
Goal~1.

\subsection{Map Quality}
\label{sec:results-map-quality}

Three reference spans were each measured two ways: the map-frame
distance between two corners, read via RViz's \texttt{Publish Point}
tool on \texttt{/clicked\_point} and a direct tape measurement of the
same real-world span. Table~\ref{tab:results-map-quality} presents all
three.

corner-picking imprecision -- the map resolution itself
(\SI{0.05}{\meter}/pixel) cannot explain a multi-decimetre discrepancy
on the longest span.

\subsection{Localisation Accuracy Ground-Truth}
\label{sec:results-localization-accuracy}

The same straight-line goal used for Goal~1 (map-frame position
$(2.0, 0.0)$, heading \ang{0}) was sent three times via the
\texttt{NavigateToPose} action client,\footnote{Sent via
\texttt{ros2 action send\_goal}, not the \texttt{ros2 topic pub
/goal\_pose} method used earlier in this project: a \texttt{BEST\_EFFORT},
no-retransmission topic combined with a short-lived one-shot publisher
was found during this session to intermittently drop the goal
entirely, with no BT tick and no planner call. The action interface's
handshaked accept/reject protocol does not share this failure mode.}
resetting the vehicle to the same start tape mark each time. AMCL's
final pose was compared against a direct physical measurement: tape
distance from the start mark and yaw derived from front/rear-wheel
lateral offsets using the wheelbase measured in
Section~\ref{sec:steering-trim} ($L = \SI{0.25725}{\meter}$), the same
principle as that section's own trim calibration.

\subsection{A LiDAR Range Calibration Curve and a Shared-Bias Hypothesis}
\label{sec:results-lidar-bias}

The growing, direction-consistent gap between Sections~\ref{sec:results-map-quality}
and~\ref{sec:results-localization-accuracy} raised a further question,
tested ad hoc within the same session: does this bias originate at the
LiDAR sensor itself or somewhere in the SLAM/mapping pipeline? What
began as a single \SI{1}{\meter} spot-check was expanded, within the
same session, into a five-point curve once the first result proved
informative. The vehicle was positioned with the LiDAR's rotation
centre at each tape-measured distance from a flat wall. The raw
\texttt{/scan} range at the beam pointing directly at the wall showed
a clean V-shaped minimum across neighbouring beams every time,
confirming genuine flat-wall geometry rather than a single noisy
reading. Table~\ref{tab:results-lidar-curve} presents all five points.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Raw LiDAR range versus tape-measured distance from a flat wall, five points.}
  \label{tab:results-lidar-curve}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Tape distance & Raw LiDAR range & Gap & \% short \\
    \midrule
    \SI{0.5}{\meter} & \SI{0.490}{\meter} & \SI{0.010}{\meter} & 1.9\% \\
    \SI{1.0}{\meter} & \SI{0.968}{\meter} & \SI{0.032}{\meter} & 3.2\% \\
    \SI{2.0}{\meter} ($\times 2$) & \SI{1.884}{\meter} / \SI{1.890}{\meter} & \SI{0.113}{\meter} avg & 5.7\% avg \\
    \SI{3.0}{\meter} & \SI{2.762}{\meter} & \SI{0.238}{\meter} & 8.0\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Neither a pure fixed-offset nor a pure fixed-percentage model fits
this curve: the percentage error grows roughly linearly with distance
(approximately $+1.9$ percentage points per metre), meaning the
absolute gap grows closer to quadratically. A least-squares fit across
all five points gives

\begin{equation}
  r_{\text{raw}} \approx 0.910 \, r_{\text{true}} + \SI{0.053}{\meter},
  \label{eq:lidar-bias-fit}
\end{equation}

i.e.\ a combined $\approx 9\%$ range-scale compression plus a small
positive fixed offset, not one or the other alone. The two
\SI{2}{\meter} repeats agree within \SI{6}{\milli\meter}, making this
a low-noise, well-characterised curve rather than a single uncertain
data point.

Taken together, all three tests point toward one shared cause rather
than three independent effects: a bias that grows as it propagates
through the pipeline:
\begin{gather*}
  \text{raw LiDAR (1.9--8.0\%, growing with range)} \\
  \Downarrow \\
  \text{SLAM-built map (3.9--6.8\%)} \\
  \Downarrow \\
  \text{AMCL-localised pose (6.7--10.6\%, growing)}
\end{gather*}
The leading hypothesis is that the LiDAR's own range-scale
nonlinearity (Equation~\ref{eq:lidar-bias-fit}) is amplified through
Cartographer's pose-graph optimisation into the larger map-scale
error, which AMCL then inherits directly, since it localises purely
within the map's own (slightly compressed) coordinate frame rather
than against any independent physical reference. This hypothesis is
not yet corrected. The session characterised and connected all
three findings and produced a real, low-noise correction curve
(Equation~\ref{eq:lidar-bias-fit}), but did not apply it to the LiDAR
driver or the map itself. The direct next step is to apply this
correction if the driver exposes a range-scale or range-offset
parameter, then re-run
Sections~\ref{sec:results-map-quality}--\ref{sec:results-localization-accuracy}
to confirm it closes the gap (Chapter~\ref{ch:summary}'s Prospects).

\paragraph{A caveat this reopens for Section~\ref{sec:goal2-curved}.}
If AMCL under-reports real distance travelled by a margin comparable
to Goal~1 and Goal~2's own position errors (\SI{0.214}{\meter} and
\SI{0.129}{\meter}), then the \texttt{GoalCritic}/\texttt{PathAlignCritic}
tension identified there may not be the sole contributor to those two
figures. Some fraction of both may instead reflect the vehicle
sitting closer to its true physical goal than AMCL's own
(scale-compressed) estimate reports. Both explanations can be true
simultaneously and are not mutually exclusive. This thesis does not
have the data to separate their individual contributions for the two
specific trials, since neither was independently
ground-truth-measured at the time. It reports both candidate causes
here rather than favouring one without evidence.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:results-limitations}

The results above are real and, where a number is given, directly
measured. But a complete picture of this thesis's final state also
requires stating plainly what was not established. Seven items
follow: five are carried forward from the sections above, the sixth
is structural to the project as a whole and the seventh is a
hardware defect observed throughout testing but never root-caused.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No systematic, pre-planned goal-reaching success-rate
    campaign across varied goal geometries.} Section~\ref{sec:results-goal-reaching}
    now has three genuine repeated-trial hardware results -- the
    K-turn goals (2/2, Section~\ref{sec:results-cusp}) and Goal~2
    repeated four times (4/4 succeeded, real variability in duration,
    cusps and position error). But these are separate,
    individually-motivated repeat sessions on specific goals, not one
    pre-planned campaign spanning a varied,
    fixed set of goal poses and geometries that would produce a single
    defensible ``X out of Y goals succeeded'' headline number for the
    vehicle as a whole. This project's hardware access concluded with
    the final session reported throughout this chapter. No
    further hardware time was available to run such a campaign. This
    remains an open limitation rather than a planned next step (see
    Chapter~\ref{ch:summary}'s Prospects for what such a campaign would
    have needed to cover).
  \item \textbf{One result predates the project's own final fixes.}
    The navigation-timing figures behind
    Section~\ref{sec:results-goal-reaching}'s caveat were measured
    before the \texttt{readMode=0} fix (Section~\ref{sec:readmode-bug}).
    This is reported as the pre-fix gap that genuinely existed at that
    point in the project, not silently updated or removed, but it has
    not been re-measured under the final configuration.
  \item \textbf{The curvature-continuing cusp refinement is
    simulation-only.} Section~\ref{sec:cusp-continued-iteration}'s
    most recent smoother refinements are, at the time of writing,
    verified only in simulation; the hardware-validated 2/2 result
    above belongs to the earlier, straight-line version of the
    smoother, not this refinement.
  \item \textbf{Low-speed closed-loop control was out of scope.}
    Section~\ref{sec:final-calibration} identified
    sub-\SI{0.25}{\meter\per\second} open-loop duty control as
    unreliable in principle, not as a remaining tuning gap -- closing
    it would require an encoder-feedback velocity loop that this
    thesis does not implement.
  \item \textbf{A map-scale and localisation bias was found and
    characterised, but not root-caused or corrected.}
    Section~\ref{sec:results-map-localization}'s ground-truth
    measurements found the saved map underestimates real distance by
    4--7\%, AMCL's reported forward distance under-reports physical
    travel by a growing 6.7--10.6\% and even the raw LiDAR range
    itself is 1.9--8.0\% short against a tape-measured reference,
    growing with distance (Equation~\ref{eq:lidar-bias-fit}). This
    looks like a single bias that grows as it propagates from sensor,
    through SLAM, to localisation, rather than three independent
    effects. The leading hypothesis (a LiDAR range-scale bias amplified
    by Cartographer's pose-graph optimisation) is not yet confirmed
    and neither the map nor the LiDAR calibration was corrected in
    response. This thesis characterises and connects the finding,
    including a low-noise correction curve, without resolving it.
  \item \textbf{The scope was navigation, not full autonomy.} This
    thesis addresses reaching a given goal pose reliably; it does not
    address perception-driven goal selection, dynamic obstacle
    avoidance beyond Nav2's stock costmap layers or multi-goal
    mission planning. Each is a substantial extension in its own
    right, named explicitly in Chapter~\ref{ch:summary}'s Prospects
    rather than left as an unstated boundary of the work.
  \item \label{item:lidar-render-freeze} \textbf{The LiDAR scan display still freezes momentarily during
    sharp turns and sudden forward acceleration.} This symptom
    recurred throughout hardware testing across this project and
    remains unresolved at the time of writing. It has not been
    root-caused. Whether it affects only the RViz visualisation or
    the underlying \texttt{/scan} stream Nav2 itself consumes is not
    established.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Applicability of the Results}
\label{sec:results-applicability}

The throttle deadband/gain values (Equation~\ref{eq:throttle-calibration})
and the steering-trim value (Section~\ref{sec:steering-trim}) are
per-unit calibration constants, expected to differ, potentially
substantially, on a different physical QCar chassis or even on
this same chassis under different tyre wear, floor surface, or
battery charge conditions. This was confirmed directly, not only in
principle, by a later hardware session, weeks after the
calibration above. Three clean, repeated \SI{2}{\meter} forward runs
at three different commanded speeds all showed a consistent
$\approx$\SI{10}{\percent} undershoot against
Equation~\ref{eq:throttle-calibration}'s prediction, so
\texttt{THROTTLE\_GAIN} was re-fitted from \num{0.070} to
\num{0.077} for that session. Reverse-direction driving showed a
further, distinct issue: stop latency and extra distance were
consistently 2--4$\times$ worse than forward's at every tested speed.
A single spot-check
(\SI{1}{\meter}~@~\SI{-0.3}{\meter\per\second}) later in the same
session came back statistically identical to forward's normal
numbers, an intermittent effect. A physical inspection (wheels,
axle couplings) found no mechanical fault, pointing instead to the
open rear differential's own load-dependent torque redistribution as
a real, physically-expected contributor to run-to-run traction
variability that no static software gain constant can fully correct
for. The \emph{method} used to find these constants is an empirical
fixed-duty sweep under real load, rather than an analytically derived
or single-point gain estimate. It is expected to transfer directly to
any similarly actuated small-scale ground vehicle, independent of the
specific numeric results.

Two further findings generalise beyond this platform and this
domain. The \texttt{readMode} buffering defect
(Section~\ref{sec:readmode-bug}) is specific to the Quanser HAL's
task-based I/O mode. The general failure class it represents is a
background acquisition task started at object-construction time,
with a read-consumption rate that happens to match its production
rate closely enough that a start-up-gap backlog never drains. This is
a generic ring-buffer hazard worth checking for in any hardware
abstraction layer offering a buffered or task-based I/O mode as an
alternative to immediate/polled reads. The diagnostic principle that
resolved it is a general debugging principle, not specific to this

for direct hardware deployment on a system with confirmed actuator

control logic through by hand for an overshoot scenario or validate
it in simulation, before it reaches a physical actuator.
