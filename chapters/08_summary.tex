\chapter{Summary and Prospects}
\label{ch:summary}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

This thesis set out to migrate a Nav2-based navigation stack for an
Ackermann-steered vehicle, the Quanser QCar, from a validated Gazebo
simulation onto the physical robot and to identify and address the
sim-to-real gap encountered along the way
(Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}). Each of the four sub-questions
posed in the introduction is answered directly by one methodology
chapter:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation-stack structure} (Chapter~\ref{ch:sim-stack}):
    the stack was migrated twice. It moved from a hand-rolled
    bicycle-model inversion to \texttt{ros2\_control}'s Ackermann
    controller, deliberately adopting real dead-reckoning odometry
    rather than simulation ground truth, so the eventual hardware
    migration would not have to absorb that discrepancy on top of
    everything else. It then moved to a direct Gazebo drive plugin.
    As Section~\ref{sec:odom-regression} documents, this plugin
    quietly reverted \texttt{/odom} back to ground truth despite the
    topic name and intent staying the same. This thesis surfaces
    that discrepancy rather than leaving it uncorrected.
  \item \textbf{Bridging the QCar's onboard compute}
    (Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware-bridge}): the vehicle's Jetson TX2 could
    not run the project's ROS~2 Humble stack natively. Its 32\,GB
    eMMC had no realistic room for a second full ROS~2 installation
    alongside its factory software. Native ROS~2 communication
    between the vehicle's onboard ROS~2 Dashing and the development
    host's ROS~2 Humble was also confirmed, through direct testing
    rather than assumption, to be impossible at the DDS protocol
    level. A TCP/JSON bridge was designed and built that
    presents the physical robot to the rest of the navigation stack
    as an ordinary ROS~2 robot. LiDAR angle-convention, odometry,
    steering-trim and inter-machine clock-synchronisation corrections
    are layered on top, bringing the bridged data to a fidelity usable
    by AMCL and Nav2.
  \item \textbf{Actuator identification and calibration}
    (Chapters~\ref{ch:hardware-bridge}--\ref{ch:calibration}): an
    uncalibrated throttle mapping (2.6--2.7$\times$ overshoot) was
    corrected to within 5\% accuracy across
    \SIrange{0.3}{0.5}{\meter\per\second} via an empirical duty sweep.
    A large ($\approx$\SI{2}{\second}) apparent coasting delay was
    ultimately traced not to physical drivetrain dynamics but to a
    ring-buffer artefact in the vendor hardware abstraction layer's
    task-based I/O mode, reducing start-up delay and stop latency by
    more than an order of magnitude once corrected. Independently,
    a real $\approx$39\% steering-gain error was characterised
    through a seven-point sweep and corrected to $\approx$5.6\%
    residual error (Section~\ref{sec:steering-gain}). This error was
    separate from the vehicle's zero-point trim and was not fixed by
    it.
  \item \textbf{Closed-loop validation} (Chapter~\ref{ch:nav-integration}):
    the bridged, calibrated vehicle was shown to complete real Nav2
    goals requiring both straight driving and turning, using a custom
    Ackermann-appropriate MPPI critic and a phased integration
    approach that isolated each subsystem before combining it with the
    next. A separate execution-side freeze at reversal cusps during
    K-turn manoeuvres in tight spaces was diagnosed to MPPI's own
    cusp-arrival gating and resolved with a custom path-smoothing
    plugin. This cut a representative manoeuvre's mean duration from
    \SI{110.2}{\second} (with recoveries) to \SI{43.9}{\second} (with
    none) in simulation. The fix was validated directly on the
    physical vehicle across two independent goals, with zero
    recoveries in either (Section~\ref{sec:cusp-freeze}).
\end{itemize}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} quantified the resulting gap. Actuator
response now tracks commanded speed within 5\% (down from a
2.6--2.7$\times$ error). Stop-latency/coast behaviour was reduced
from roughly 2.4\,s and up to 3$\times$ the commanded stop distance,
to approximately 0.5\,s and $\approx$\SI{0.1}{\meter}. This residual
is fully explained by ordinary linear velocity decay, not by any
remaining unexplained mechanism.

\subsection{Critical Reflection}
\label{sec:critical-reflection}

What worked well in this project was the discipline of measuring
directly on hardware, rather than trusting a plausible-sounding
explanation. This thesis's four most significant findings are the
protocol-version incompatibility in
Section~\ref{sec:rmw-incompatibility}, the sim-vs-real overshoot
ratio in Section~\ref{sec:sim-vs-real}, the buffering defect in
Section~\ref{sec:readmode-bug} and the reversal-cusp freeze in
Section~\ref{sec:cusp-freeze}. Each was, at some point, plausibly
attributable to a simpler cause: localisation error, a
request-delivery bug, physical coasting, or a tuning-only gain
problem. Direct measurement subsequently ruled each of these out.

The cusp-freeze investigation additionally illustrates the same
discipline applied to solutions, not only diagnoses. Three plausible
fix attempts and two later follow-on refinements were each tested
directly against hardware or a controlled reproduction. Each was
found wanting by measurement rather than intuition, then reverted.
The evidence that ruled it out was recorded, not discarded.
What could have gone better is
equally clear from Section~\ref{sec:active-braking}: a reactive
control design was deployed to physical hardware before it had been
adequately traced through or simulated. It produced a genuine
safety incident. The incident causing no lasting harm does not
make the shortcut it resulted from acceptable practice. This
thesis records it, unedited, as a documented counter-example to its
own otherwise measurement-first methodology.

A second, more structural limitation is that not every quantitative
result in this thesis is a genuinely final, post-fix measurement.
Not every claim of success, either, is backed by a repeated-trial
statistic rather than a qualitative assessment.
Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations} states these gaps in full, in
one place, rather than leaving them scattered across individual
chapters. Most notably, no systematic goal-reaching success-rate
campaign was run. Two results -- navigation timing and the
sim-versus-real comparison -- predate the project's own final fixes.
These are reported as open items rather
than papered over. The exact next steps to close each of them are
specific and immediately actionable rather than vague, as the
Prospects below make concrete.

\section{Prospects}
\label{sec:prospects}

Each limitation named in Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations}
motivates at least one item below. This project's hardware access
concluded with the 2026-09-10 session reported throughout
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Every item below is accordingly a direction
for continuation work -- by this project or a successor -- rather than
a next step this thesis itself still has the means to take:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Re-running the two pre-fix measurements.}
    \texttt{monitor\_goal\_timing.py} (Section~\ref{sec:display-lag})
    and the six-point sim-versus-real sweep
    (Section~\ref{sec:results-sim-vs-real}) were both measured before
    the \texttt{readMode=0} fix. The sweep was also measured before
    throttle calibration. Both would need a further QCar lab session
    to re-run under the final configuration, closing
    Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations}'s item~2 with genuine
    post-fix numbers instead of a qualitative confirmation.
  \item \textbf{A planned, repeated goal-reaching evaluation
    campaign.} Closing Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations}'s item~1
    directly, a fixed set of goal poses spanning straight-line,
    gentle-turn and reversal geometries would each be repeated across
    multiple trials. This would produce the single defensible
    goal-reaching success-rate figure this thesis currently lacks.
    This figure would be the natural companion to the K-turn-specific
    2/2 hardware result already in hand (Section~\ref{sec:results-cusp}).
  \item \textbf{Correcting the map/localisation scale bias.}
    Section~\ref{sec:results-map-localization}'s ground-truth
    benchmarking (closing Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations}'s
    item~5) found a real, direction-consistent bias: map $-$4--7\%,
    AMCL forward distance $-$6.7--10.6\% and growing, raw LiDAR range
    $-$1.9--8.0\% and growing with distance. It characterised this
    bias with a low-noise, five-point LiDAR calibration curve
    (Equation~\ref{eq:lidar-bias-fit}), but did not apply any
    correction. If the LiDAR driver exposes a range-scale or
    range-offset parameter, applying Equation~\ref{eq:lidar-bias-fit}'s
    fit and re-running
    Sections~\ref{sec:results-map-quality}--\ref{sec:results-localization-accuracy}
    would show directly whether correcting the sensor closes the
    larger map- and AMCL-level gaps, or whether a second, independent
    effect inside Cartographer's pose-graph optimisation also
    contributes.
  \item \textbf{Closed-loop low-speed control.} This thesis's
    calibration (Section~\ref{sec:final-calibration}) explicitly
    identified the sub-\SI{0.25}{\meter\per\second} regime as a hard
    limit of open-loop duty control, not a remaining tuning gap.
    Reliable low-speed creep -- useful for close-quarters manoeuvring
    or fine final-approach positioning -- would require closed-loop
    velocity feedback from the encoder rather than the fixed duty
    mapping used throughout this thesis.
  \item \textbf{Deceleration-profile tuning for coast reduction.} The
    stop-latency residual characterised in Section~\ref{sec:results-stop-distance}
    is now small, but not zero and scales with cruise speed. Given
    that active braking is explicitly ruled out on safety grounds
    (Section~\ref{sec:active-braking}), a lower-risk avenue is tuning
    the MPPI \texttt{GoalCritic}/deceleration profile in
    \texttt{nav2\_params.yaml} so the controller commands a lower
    cruise speed during final approach to a goal. This was identified
    but not investigated within this thesis. It would proportionally
    shrink, not eliminate, the coast without introducing any new
    reactive control loop.
  \item \textbf{Generalising the diagnostic methodology.} This
    thesis's most transferable contribution may be procedural rather
    than numeric: an internally self-consistent software abstraction
    can mask a stale or defective input (Section~\ref{sec:readmode-bug}).
    The specific discipline of verifying a component's own upstream
    input, not only its internal consistency, is a general principle
    worth testing on other buffered or task-based hardware interfaces
    beyond the one vendor library examined here.
  \item \textbf{Closing the simulated-versus-real replan-complexity gap.}
    Section~\ref{sec:cusp-hw-validation} found that real-hardware K-turn
    manoeuvres occasionally produce substantially higher per-replan
    cusp counts (8--10) than any simulation trial reproduced (maximum
    $\approx$3), without preventing either tested goal from succeeding.
    The leading candidate explanations were identified but not
    isolated and remain open. One is \texttt{SmacPlannerHybrid}'s
    sensitivity to real, more awkward goal/environment geometry. The
    other is a residual, session-specific clock-offset effect similar
    to the one implicated in Section~\ref{sec:cusp-followon-reverted}'s
    reverted \texttt{IsPathValidDebounced} attempt. Relatedly, a
    deviation-aware critic weighting scheme was proposed but not
    implemented, as a controller-side complement to
    Section~\ref{sec:cusp-freeze}'s path-preprocessing fix. The scheme
    would ease a sampled trajectory's pull back toward the exact
    planned path once it has deviated significantly, favouring a
    goal-direction target instead of insisting on precise
    reconvergence.
  \item \textbf{Hardware re-validation of the curvature-continuing
    smoother refinement.} Section~\ref{sec:cusp-continued-iteration}'s
    two most recent refinements are the \texttt{min\_lead\_distance}
    fix to the cusp re-extension bug and the curvature-continuing
    extension itself. At the time of writing, both were verified only
    in simulation: a single smoke test for the curvature refinement
    and two trials without directly observing the skip branch fire
    for \texttt{min\_lead\_distance}. Both are strict generalisations
    of the already hardware-validated straight-line mechanism and are
    expected to transfer. This thesis deliberately does not claim
    hardware confirmation it does not have. Repeating
    Section~\ref{sec:cusp-hw-validation}'s two-goal real-vehicle test
    against this refined configuration is the immediate next step.
  \item \textbf{IMU/odometry sensor fusion.} This thesis's pose
    estimation between LiDAR updates relies solely on wheel-encoder
    odometry (Section~\ref{sec:qcar-platform}); the QCar's onboard IMU
    is present and readable through the vendor HAL/PAL library but was
    never read by this project's software stack. Fusing the two via
    an extended or unscented Kalman filter, for example the ROS~2
    \texttt{robot\_localization} package, is a specified but unbuilt
    extension: IMU and wheel odometry in, a filtered
    \texttt{odom}$\rightarrow$\texttt{base\_link} transform out,
    feeding AMCL exactly as the current unfiltered odometry does. It
    was considered during this project but not implemented.
  \item \textbf{Beyond navigation.} This thesis's scope, stated
    explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations}'s final item,
    is reaching a given goal pose reliably. It does not address how
    that goal pose is chosen, how the vehicle would react to an
    obstacle Nav2's stock costmap layers do not already handle, or
    sequencing multiple goals into a mission. This thesis's navigation
    pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:qcar-platform}) uses only the 2D LiDAR;
    no camera-based perception and no IMU data was used or needed for
    pose-goal navigation. Perception-driven goal selection, dynamic
    obstacle handling beyond the static/inflation costmap layers used
    throughout and a mission-level task layer above
    \texttt{navigate\_to\_pose} are the three most direct extensions
    of this work into full autonomy. Each is substantial enough to be
    its own follow-on project rather than a tuning task.
  \item \textbf{Diagnosing the LiDAR scan-rendering freeze.}
    Section~\ref{sec:results-limitations}'s item~7 records a scan
    display freeze during sharp turns and sudden forward acceleration
    that recurred throughout hardware testing but was never
    root-caused. The immediate next step is determining whether the
    freeze is confined to the RViz visualisation pipeline or also
    affects the \texttt{/scan} stream Nav2's own controller consumes,
    since only the latter would be a genuine navigation-accuracy
    concern rather than a cosmetic one.
\end{itemize}

The QCar's navigation stack, as delivered by this thesis, reaches
real closed-loop navigation goals on physical hardware with a
sim-to-real gap that is now measured, explained and in most respects
substantially reduced -- the original goal stated in
Section~\ref{sec:problem-statement}. What remains open is narrower
and better characterised than what this thesis began with: not
why the system does not work, but two specific, already-scoped
measurements and two specific, already-identified directions for
further improvement.
